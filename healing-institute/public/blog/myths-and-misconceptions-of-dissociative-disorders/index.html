Myths and Misconceptions of Dissociative Disorders

Brian RobertsonMSW, LCSW, EMDR-C

Dissociative disorders are among the most misunderstood conditions in mental health. Popular media often portrays dissociation, particularly Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID), as dramatic, bizarre, or inherently dangerous. These portrayals contribute to stigma and can make it harder for people experiencing dissociative symptoms to recognize what is happening or seek appropriate treatment.

The purpose of this post is to outline the most common myths and misconceptions about dissociative disorders and replace them with facts.

Myth #1: Dissociative disorders are rare.

It has often been said that dissociative disorders are extremely rare. Even some in the scientific community say so. While dissociative disorders are very hard to diagnose, data show they aren’t as rare as people think. Most studies put the prevalence of dissociative disorders in the general population at around 10%, or about 1 in every 10 people. Considering that dissociative disorders result from complex trauma, and complex trauma isn’t rare, this makes sense.

Myth #2: Dissociative disorders are obvious and easily noticed.

Dissociative disorders aren’t easily seen and are very hard to diagnose. TV and movies portray someone with a dissociative disorder, usually DID, as having “multiple personalities” and completely switching character in front of others. That’s just for TV. In real life, only about 4–6% of people with DID present with overt symptoms of self-state switching, and even then it is hard for most people to notice.

Dissociative disorders are also commonly misdiagnosed as other conditions like bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, borderline personality disorder, or OCD. In fact, most people with a dissociative disorder spend about 5–7 years in the mental health system before receiving the proper diagnosis.

Myth #3: People with dissociative disorders have “split personalities.”

People with dissociative disorders don’t have split personalities. Think about it this way: you are not the same person as a co-worker, a friend, a sibling, a partner, or a parent. And yet these are all different parts of you that make up who you are, and it all feels like you.

For someone with a dissociative disorder, those parts feel separate. Sometimes they are even separated by amnesia; other times they aren’t. Each part has a purpose behind its existence, because it was necessary for survival during times of trauma. For example, there may be a part whose job is to hold the trauma memories and keep them separate from consciousness, or a protective part whose job is to keep us from discovering those memories.

Myth #4: Anyone can develop a dissociative disorder at any time.

Several factors make someone susceptible to developing a dissociative disorder: there must be complex childhood trauma, there must be multiple traumatic events, and those events start before age 6 (on average, before age 4). The type of trauma matters too. For example, while childhood emotional abuse can be traumatizing, it has a lower correlation with dissociative disorders than physical and sexual abuse.

A dissociative disorder develops during childhood as a protective mechanism to survive the trauma. This means someone cannot develop a dissociative disorder like DID or OSDD as an adult.

Myth #5: People with dissociative disorders are dangerous.

People with dissociative disorders are no more dangerous than anyone else in the general population. This myth likely comes from depictions on TV and in movies. Because of re-traumatization, people with dissociative disorders are far more likely to be victims than perpetrators.

If you are interested in learning more about dissociative disorders, you can visit did-research.org and isst-d.org.
