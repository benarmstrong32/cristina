Can trauma cause Functional Neurological Disorder (FND)?

Brian RobertsonMSW, LCSW, EMDR-C

The short answer: it’s possible. The truth is, we don’t fully know what causes it. Many people with Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) have no history of complex trauma. However, plenty of studies show a correlation between complex childhood trauma and FND. One study found a correlation between adverse childhood experiences and FND symptom severity. But before we answer whether trauma can cause FND, let’s first talk about what it is.

What is Functional Neurological Disorder?

Functional Neurological Disorder, or FND, is a neurological condition caused by changes in how the brain works. This is different from other neurological disorders that involve changes to the brain’s structure itself. With FND, there are changes in the connections between different brain regions. Think of it as different parts of the brain that aren’t communicating well with each other, rather than a physical alteration to the brain.

A person with FND can have a wide variety of physical and mental symptoms, which may include:

  • Seizure-like episodes
  • Movement problems
  • Problems with cognitive functioning
  • Problems with vision or hearing
  • Dissociative symptoms
  • Involuntary muscle movements or spasms
  • Numbness
  • Pain
  • Dysautonomic issues

FND usually requires a neurologist to diagnose. It is hard to diagnose and often requires a lot of testing to rule out other conditions. Symptoms can present at any time in life. In my experience as a therapist, when FND does have links to trauma, it often presents during times of high stress or when a trigger related to a past traumatic event is present. In many cases, symptoms are not constant. Most are short-lived, going dormant until there is another trigger.

Interoception and FND

One thing FND seems to impair is interoception: our brain and nervous system’s ability to sense, interpret, and integrate signals from inside the body. It allows us to be aware of internal states such as hunger, thirst, temperature, and pain. Interoception also helps the body maintain homeostasis by allowing the brain to communicate with other organs, for example regulating blood pressure as we stand up.

Interoception is also responsible for awareness of our thoughts and emotions. Think of it as the hub for interpreting and integrating all the signals from our body, along with our thoughts and emotions. This is especially true because all emotions have a physical “felt sense.” In FND, symptoms can sometimes be brought on by intense emotions.

Interoception is controlled by a part of the brain called the insula, or insular cortex. Many things can cause a disconnect or dysregulation between the insula and other areas of the brain, in turn affecting interoception. One thing we know impacts the insula and interoception is trauma history. Does this mean trauma causes FND? Not necessarily, but let’s examine other links between trauma and FND.

The link between dissociative disorders and FND

Research over the years points to high comorbidity between FND and dissociative disorders. A dissociative disorder is an ongoing condition where a person’s consciousness becomes detached from certain experiences, thoughts, emotions, memories, sensations, or even aspects of their identity. Dissociative disorders are caused by extensive complex childhood trauma, usually starting at a young age. It was the brain’s way of adapting and surviving during those years, and it continues because that part of the brain doesn’t realize the trauma is over.

While it’s possible to have a dissociative disorder without FND, and FND without a dissociative disorder, comorbidity between the two may be as high as 80%. Dissociation is thought to be one of the main mechanisms behind loss of interoception and possibly many FND symptoms. One study found that DES-II scores (used to measure dissociation) in FND were nearly as high as those in dissociative disorders.

Conclusions

While there is no way to say definitively whether trauma is the origin or cause of FND, there are some compelling links. It makes sense that complex trauma, already known to cause significant dissociative symptoms, could change how neurons connect through the impairment of interoception. We already know from the ACE (Adverse Childhood Experiences) study that complex trauma greatly increases the risk of developing many physiological issues. If some of those issues are neurological in nature, why couldn’t trauma be a cause?

And if trauma is the cause, does that mean FND symptoms can be resolved? What I can say is that, in my experience, where there is a link between FND symptoms and someone’s trauma history, resolving the trauma can have a profound effect on existing FND symptoms.
