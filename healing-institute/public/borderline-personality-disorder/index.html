What is Borderline Personality Disorder?

Borderline Personality Disorder, or BPD, is a mental health condition characterized by emotional dysregulation, an unstable or absent sense of self, difficulties with interpersonal relationships, and heightened sensitivity to perceived or real rejection or abandonment. The prevalence of BPD is thought to be around 6% of the general population.

What causes BPD?

Through the lens of structural dissociation, BPD appears to be caused by complex childhood trauma and attachment injury, or attachment wounding. This is consistent with most people with BPD having some dissociative amnesia from childhood.

The symptoms and behaviors of BPD are the result of a survival mechanism: the mind’s way of trying to survive complex trauma in childhood. They are not character flaws.

Take, for example, an unstable or absent sense of self. This is caused by traumatic dissociation and parts activity, commonly found in people with complex trauma. Another example is heightened sensitivity to perceived or real rejection or abandonment. In terms of attachment injury, this would have been important for recognizing danger in traumatic situations.

Signs and symptoms

Tap any sign below to read real-life examples. Recognizing yourself in some of these is not a diagnosis, but it can be a helpful starting point for a conversation.

Sign 1 of 9Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment

Examples: Becoming highly distressed when someone cancels plans, making drastic attempts to keep someone from leaving a relationship, preemptively withdrawing emotionally to reduce the anticipated pain of abandonment or rejection, excessively seeking reassurance, or becoming angry when someone appears distant.

Sign 2 of 9Unstable, intense relationships

Examples: Rapidly shifting from viewing someone as wonderful to viewing them as terrible, abruptly ending relationships after conflict, quickly entering relationships that feel intense and are short-lived, or dramatic shifts in how we view someone without any obvious change in their behavior.

Sign 3 of 9Unstable self-image or sense of self

Examples: Frequently changing plans for the future, feeling like your identity changes based on the situation and who is present, difficulty identifying preferences because of automatically taking on someone else’s, internal conflict about goals and values, or frequently changing career, educational, or life goals.

Sign 4 of 9Impulsivity in potentially self-damaging areas

Examples: Substance use, impulsive spending, gambling, binge eating, unsafe sexual behavior, or abruptly making major life decisions without considering the consequences.

Sign 5 of 9Recurrent suicidal behavior, threats, or self-harm

Examples: Suicide attempts, recurrent suicidal threats, internal suicidal fantasies, or self-harm through cutting, burning, etc. If you are in danger right now, please call or text 988 or call 911.

Sign 6 of 9Affect (mood) instability

Examples: Rapid mood shifts following interpersonal triggers, disproportionate responses to perceived rejection, emotional flooding followed by rapid shifts back to baseline, or abrupt internal transitions from attachment and security to fear or rage.

Sign 7 of 9Chronic feelings of emptiness

Examples: Feeling empty or hollow, repeatedly seeking stimulation or relationships to escape emptiness, difficulty tolerating being alone, feeling like something is fundamentally missing from your life, or chronically feeling blank, numb, or unreal.

Sign 8 of 9Intense or hard-to-control anger

Examples: Frequent arguments, explosive anger, rumination about perceived mistreatment, rapid internal escalation in response to perceived rejection, or self-directed anger replacing outwardly expressed anger.

Sign 9 of 9Stress-related paranoia or severe dissociation

Examples: Memory gaps associated with stressful situations, internally questioning everyone’s motives, becoming suspicious of others conspiring against you during times of stress, feeling detached from your body, depersonalization/derealization, or experiencing yourself as “not me.”

Treatment options

One of the most effective treatments for BPD is Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT. It is especially helpful for stabilizing our lives and helping us respond better to the events in them. People with BPD can often feel like they are spinning their wheels, and DBT is designed to help with that by focusing on four skill areas:

  • Distress tolerance
  • Emotion regulation
  • Mindfulness
  • Interpersonal effectiveness

For the complex trauma elements, modalities like Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy, Brainspotting, or Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) can be helpful.
